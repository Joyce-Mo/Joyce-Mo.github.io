---
title: The Chinese Creation Story
description: How Chinese creation myths connect the act of creation to reducing chaos to order, from Hun-tun to Pangu.
tags: [mythology, chinese culture]
---

A theme persisting throughout Chinese thought is the connection between creation and the act of reducing chaos to order. Several stories persist as the "creation myth" within Chinese ideology, but one that stands out to me concerns Hu, the emperor of the North Sea, and Shu, the emperor of the Southern Sea.

Hu and Shu used to meet halfway between their respective domains on occasion, on the territory of the Centre emperor, Hun-tun. In the lore, Hun-tun is characterized by the lack of 7 orifices: seeing, hearing, eating, and breathing. Shu and Hu gave Hun-tun a gift as gratitude for allowing them to meet—at the rate of one new hole a day. On the 7th day, Hun-tun died, and the world came into being. Note that Hun-tun means chaos in traditional Chinese. The combined name of the 2 emperors, Shutu, means lightning, hinting that it is the lightning that strikes Chaos and destroys it for the world to emerge. Artistically, this is often represented by lightning directed at a goat skin bottle for chaos.

Another fundamental concept in Chinese thought is chaos separating into an initial yin and yang.

Another story speaks of an ancient ruler named Panku (also known as Pangu), often cited as the closest version of a creation myth in Taoist legends. Pangu, the first man, is said to have been created out of the cosmic egg and had two horns, two tusks, and a hairy body. According to the ancient myths, Pangu is said to have shaped the earth by chiseling out valleys and stacking up mountains. The concept of yin and yang is still present in this story, such that Pangu acts on the belief of yin and yang, and chaos and separation.
